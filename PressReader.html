How public libraries can support media literacy in their communities

Ask public library staff whether media literacy is a part of their job, and almost all of them will say yes. But if you ask them whether their library is well equipped to support media literacy efforts, very few will agree.

Patrons are already bringing their media literacy questions to the library: a video that seems too perfect to be real, two news reports telling the same story in different ways, an opinion piece that feels like news. So that gap matters.

Based on feedback from libraries worldwide, this piece looks at current library media literacy work, what is succeeding and how to extend it.

How do libraries support media literacy?

Mostly through one-to-one help at the desk or by appointment, plus workshops on scams and AI content, access to reliable sources and partnerships with schools and community groups.

Why libraries have an important role in fostering media literacy

Patrons already ask staff for help with information, and staff can coach them to judge sources without dictating conclusions.

Staff see the work as theirs. In PressReader’s research with ALIA, 99% of Australian public library staff called media literacy an important responsibility and 60% called it extremely important; in the US and Canada, 88% of staff surveyed with PLA rated it very or extremely important.

Capacity lags: only 10% of Australian respondents felt very well equipped, and over 90% in the US and Canada said they fall short of their goals.

What media literacy support looks like in libraries

Most of it sits outside formal programs and typically includes:

  • Informal help at the service desk
  • One-to-one guidance or bookable appointments
  • Workshops and group learning
  • Scam and misinformation awareness activities
  • Help using devices, platforms and digital resources
  • Curated access to trusted news platforms like PressReader
  • Dedicated research databases
  • Programs delivered with schools and community organizations

Academic libraries do similar work by teaching students to weigh sources and viewpoints.

Which library media literacy initiatives have the most impact?

The most common methods aren’t always the strongest. Australian staff in the ALIA report singled out three.

One-to-one support that meets people where they are

Personal help best reaches older adults, less confident users and patrons with limited English.

Small-group sessions grounded in real situations

Practical workshops on scams and AI, with hands-on comparison of real stories, worked well; some AI sessions drew strong turnout.

Trusted journalism used as a teaching tool

Comparing credible coverage across regions and viewpoints helped patrons see the full picture.

Practical ways libraries can strengthen media literacy

PressReader’s Media Literacy Toolkit for Public Libraries sets out five pillars drawn from the ALIA and PLA research:

1. Build staff confidence

Training was the top priority in both regions; staff should guide patrons through evidence rather than rule on what is true.

2. Recognize everyday learning opportunities

Questions about scam texts, unknown websites, conflicting articles or possible AI images are all chances to coach.

3. Use quality journalism as a teaching tool

Comparison needs range: 99% of Australian staff value diverse quality news, yet only 22% offer 30 or more sources.

4. Work with community partners

Schools, local news outlets, banks and consumer groups add expertise and reach.

5. Document the work you’re already doing

Logging information requests alongside attendance shows demand to boards, councils and funders; the Media Literacy Toolkit has a template.

Media literacy programs for libraries

Programs let patrons practise together, and the toolkit offers three simple ones:

  • Real or AI? A group game judging real and AI-made images.
  • Spot the Scam. Unpacking genuine scam messages, ideal for older patrons.
  • Compare the Headlines. One story read across several outlets.

Building media literacy in your library

This is already everyday library work, and libraries need support to keep it up as media changes.

Full survey findings are in Ready to Act (ALIA) and News Access and Media Literacy in Public Libraries (PLA).
