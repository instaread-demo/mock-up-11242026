When Don DeLillo Wrote Hockey Erotica

In “Amazons,” an “intimate memoir” by a female hockey player, women win the battle of the sexes on the ice and in bed.

By S. C. Cornell

November 23, 2026

The first rule of the sports novel is that it is not actually about sports. In most cases, it’s about sacrifice or greatness—themes of personal interest to writers, who, like athletes, could usually find an easier way to make a living. But writing is hard to dramatize, and for the bespectacled dweeb in the small room who spends his days backspacing and muttering and making trips to the fridge just to stare into its faint light without seeing anything in particular, the tendency is to transpose professional anxiety to flashier arenas.

Don DeLillo’s first try at the form, and only his second novel, came in 1972. “End Zone” follows a college football player fixated on nuclear-age violence and wonders whether the brutality of war, or of the game, can be justified by the meaning its participants find in it. It ends with two teammates, one of them the squad’s first Black player, talking about giving the sport up in a nearly empty room.

Cornell calls it one of DeLillo’s weaker books, but sees in that last scene an early taste for chaos theory and for setting vast stakes against tiny details.

He came back to sport in 1997 with “Underworld,” a baseball epic of more than eight hundred pages that is really about ordinary people and the ordinariness of famous ones. A line in “Libra” about a Kennedy-assassination researcher whose true subject turns out to be men in small rooms reads, Cornell suggests, as DeLillo describing himself.

The least-known of his sports novels starts instead with women in a room. “Amazons: An Intimate Memoir by the First Woman Ever to Play in the National Hockey League” came out in 1980 under the name Cleo Birdwell, its supposed heroine; DeLillo acknowledged it only recently and, at ninety, has agreed to a reissue, though fans long recognized his voice. In 1992, David Foster Wallace, whose “Infinite Jest” would lean heavily on DeLillo’s sports writing, sent him an admiring letter that mentioned hearing the Birdwell book had outsold everything DeLillo wrote before “White Noise.”

The source of that tip was Gerald Howard, a DeLillo editor at Viking, who later confirmed it in a piece for Bookforum. So until DeLillo was forty-eight, his biggest seller was a mock memoir of the kind now labelled hockey erotica.

The book skips the struggle and sacrifice its subtitle implies; it is a sex comedy about men and women in close quarters. Howard says DeLillo wrote it with Sue Buck, an ad writer and former colleague who contributed hockey know-how and the comic rural backstory of Cleo’s hometown, the fictional Badger, Ohio. Cornell guesses the pair wanted to explore male desire from a woman’s vantage, and a long N.H.L. road trip supplied the setting.

Its theme is that athletes have bodies. Most chapters are crisp conversations ending in a new romance, a pattern Cleo notices but never quite breaks.

Cleo is a wholesome Midwestern type surrounded by a comic gallery of men: an anxious Ivy League general manager, a Québécois coach who mainly wants someone to hear his French, a tennis player, a sportswriter named Murray Jay Siskind who later turns up in “White Noise,” and a faded player who agrees to an induced coma. She never dates teammates.

Where erotica usually turns on power, Cleo simply has all of it, and her place on the ice is never in doubt.

The humor comes largely from men’s helpless longing; harassment is played as male haplessness. When a Saudi takes over the team, a trend DeLillo foresaw, and a veil is floated, she dismisses the idea with one dry question.

On the ice, Cleo shows that being targeted has nothing to do with gender: the crowd demands a sacrificial hit, targets rotate, and her turn comes, which she wryly counts as progress for feminism.

On one road trip, a broadcaster named Toby Scott slips into Cleo’s hotel room uninvited. The book treats him as pitiful rather than menacing; Cleo quickly takes charge, and he ends up thanking her for helping him escape.

Cornell reads these scenes as a male fantasy of being forgiven, but notes that DeLillo also imagines women fantasizing about male weakness: Cleo’s deepest bond is with the man in the coma, whom she helps care for, and her agent, Floss, wishes she had a silent, sleeping boyfriend of her own.

Reading the book soon after John Giorno’s memoir “Great Demon Kings,” Cornell finds DeLillo’s far better written but similar in its interest in male frailty, and calls it a breezy forerunner of Gary Indiana’s “Rent Boy.”

The Times reported that the reissue followed the excitement over “Heated Rivalry” and demand for hockey romance, a commercial motive Cornell would rather not believe.

Either way, Cornell is glad it is back: buoyant and plainly enjoyable, a book with no real payoff that still satisfies, even if it teaches little about womanhood, greatness, or hockey.
