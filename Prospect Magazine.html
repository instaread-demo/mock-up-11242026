The Brexiteers are back—the right-wing press can’t fall for their nonsense again

As Andy Burnham tries to repair the damage done by Brexit, the media must do a better job of interrogating the claims of Leavers

By Alan Rusbridger
November 21, 2026

Like stragglers from a different age, the old Brexit crew is trying to get the band together for one last show. They are dusting off the fading playbook, strumming the strings to see if they can recall the chords. Trying to remember why they fell out with the drummer. Arguing about who gets to sing lead vocals. Mistaking the echo from the amplifiers for the roar of the crowd.

Rusbridger argues the group has convinced itself it was early rather than wrong, though the 2016 promises of sovereignty, growth, free trade and lower immigration look very different a decade on.

His sharper criticism, though, is aimed at the newspapers covering them.

As the prime minister opens a tentative conversation about ties with Europe, he says parts of Fleet Street are replaying the old routines: headlines crying betrayal, the Express warning of migrants, and its Sunday sister splashing a “shock analysis” that households would be £6,500 poorer.

That figure came from the Growth Commission, convened by Liz Truss in July 2023, nicknamed “Truss’s Taskforce” and widely seen as an attempt to revive her 2022 mini-budget agenda. Rusbridger adds that Tory peer Simon Heffer called her recent book the most “deranged” he had reviewed.

The 176-page report, promoted by Truss, appears to be chiefly the work of Shanker Singham, prompting Rusbridger to ask who funds the commission and what qualifies its author. Its backers are undisclosed; in July 2023 journalist Peter Geoghegan labelled it a “dark money outfit” tied to thinktanks with fossil fuel, tobacco and Koch funding.

He also cites a November 2018 Guardian report that Singham, who reluctantly voted Remain, has no formal economics degree and studied chemistry, not the subjects on an old Facebook profile.

With trust in news falling, Rusbridger argues, journalism must help readers test startling claims, practising what US writer Tom Rosenstiel calls “the discipline of verification”, rather than follow a partisan British tradition that tells readers what they want to hear.

His example is last week’s launch of the campaign group Stay Out, broken by Tim Shipman in the Spectator with a headline putting the cost of rejoining the EU at £750m a week. Rusbridger notes the group is run by Oliver Lewis, author of the 2016 battle bus’s £350m figure, that the piece quoted Shipman’s editor Michael Gove in support, and that the campaign video is credited to OldQueenTV, while the magazine sits on Old Queen Street.

When Rusbridger put the £750m and £6,500 claims to AI chatbots, Grok, ChatGPT and Claude each called them misleading or contested and set out several objections.

If editors simply repeat lobbyists’ talking points and leave the checking to chatbots, Rusbridger concludes, journalism will not deserve to survive; the press should scrutinise the band rather than cheer it on.

Alan Rusbridger was editor of Prospect from 2021 to 2025 and is the former head of Lady Margaret Hall, Oxford. He was editor of the Guardian from 1995 to 2015
